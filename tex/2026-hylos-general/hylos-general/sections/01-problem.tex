  \end{onlyenv}
  \par
  \begin{onlyenv}<3->
    \begin{center}
      Are they equal? Can we \emph{derive} the second from the first?
    \end{center}
  \end{onlyenv}
  \par
  \speakernote{A toy example that runs through the talk. The specification
    is a pipeline: sort, then double every element. Two passes, and an
    intermediate list that is built and thrown away. [click] The program:
    quicksort that doubles each pivot as it places it. One pass. [click]
    They compute the same thing, but convincing yourself takes thought, and
    real optimisations are much less obvious than this. Goal: derive the
    program from the specification so that equality is guaranteed.}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Three Ways to Fast and Correct Code}
  \begin{center}
    \renewcommand\arraystretch{1.6}
    \begin{tabular}{p{.27\textwidth}p{.31\textwidth}p{.31\textwidth}}
      \textbf{Approach} & \textbf{Good} & \textbf{But} \\ \hline
      \uncover<1->{Write it, then \textbf{verify} it} &
        \uncover<1->{any program} &
        \uncover<1->{\textcolor{bad}{a new proof for every program}} \\
      \uncover<2->{\textbf{Verified compiler}} &
        \uncover<2->{translation is correct} &
        \uncover<2->{\textcolor{bad}{keeps your algorithm:
          $O(n^3)$ stays $O(n^3)$}} \\
      \uncover<3->{\textbf{Calculate} it from a spec} &
        \uncover<3->{\textcolor{good}{proof comes with the derivation}} &
        \uncover<3->{needs laws that \emph{apply}} \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \speakernote{Verify after the fact: write the clever program, then prove
    it matches a specification (e.g. with Dafny, Why3, Rocq). Works for
    anything, but each program needs its own proof, often harder than the
    program. [click] Verified compilers (CompCert, CakeML) guarantee the
    translation is faithful, but they do not change your algorithm: a cubic
    program stays cubic. [click] Calculation: the optimisation is a
    sequence of steps, each an instance of a general law that was proved
    once. The proof is a by-product of the derivation. The catch: you need
    a library of laws that are general enough to apply to real programs.
    That is where recursion schemes come in.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{You Already Trust Rewrite Rules}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[alt={Map fusion: a pipeline that applies g to every
        element and then f to every element is rewritten into a single
        pipeline stage that applies f after g to every element.},
      stage/.style = {box, minimum height = 9mm, minimum width = 18mm}]
      \node[stage] (g) {map $g$};
      \node[stage, right = 6mm of g] (f) {map $f$};
      \draw[->, thick] (g) -- (f);
      \node[right = 10mm of f, font = \Large] (eq) {$\Longrightarrow$};
      \node[hot, minimum height = 9mm, right = 10mm of eq] (fg)
        {map $(f \circ g)$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \begin{itemize}[<+->]
    \item ML compilers \emph{fuse} tensor operations into one kernel.
    \item Database engines rewrite query plans.
    \item Compilers inline, fuse loops, remove temporary data.
    \item Usually: a \textbf{fixed} set of rules, \textbf{trusted}, not
      proved.
  \end{itemize}
  \speakernote{Program calculation is not exotic. Tensor compilers (XLA,
    TVM) fuse element-wise operations so that intermediate tensors never
    reach memory. Query optimisers push selections through joins. GHC has
    user-written rewrite rules for list fusion. All of these are equations
    between programs. [click] But the rules are hand-written for a fixed
    set of operators and are trusted: a wrong rule silently miscompiles
    (compiler testing keeps finding such bugs, e.g. Csmith, PLDI 2011). We
    want rules for general recursive programs, each one proved.}
\end{frame}
